Public Mechanics

A practice framework for understanding how public systems actually work

The Work That Keeps Things Running

Every day, case workers bypass broken eligibility systems to help clients access housing. Bus drivers wait an extra thirty seconds because they know someone elderly depends on that route. Teachers feed kids from personal supplies when meal programs fail. IT contractors patch the same broken permissions matrix weekly because procurement won't fund an audit.

This is repair work. It's also design work—the kind that happens after launch, when systems meet reality and start breaking. Public Mechanics is a framework for recognizing this work, understanding how it functions, and building institutional capacity to support it.

How Systems Actually Function

Most systems don't fail cleanly. They degrade through accumulated neglect, policy contradictions, and the gap between how things are supposed to work and how they actually do. The people keeping these systems functional—frontline workers, operations staff, local administrators—develop sophisticated practices for working around institutional constraints.

This local knowledge is often invisible to the people who design, fund, or govern these systems. It rarely shows up in documentation, strategic plans, or design artifacts. Yet it's essential for delivery.

Public Mechanics treats this knowledge as a core design material. Understanding how case workers actually process applications, how drivers manage routes under real conditions, how teachers navigate supply chain failures—this is systems knowledge that informs more resilient design.

Repair as Practice

Repair work requires coordination. Someone notices a problem, escalates it, secures permission to act, finds resources to fix it. Someone communicates changes, updates documentation, trains the next person. Every intervention creates coordination costs.

These costs reflect who has power in your organization. Who has authority to stop a process? Who can override a rule? Who gets listened to when something breaks? Design decisions about workflows, roles, and approval chains directly shape whether repair work is possible.

Treating repair as practice means designing organizations that can adapt under operational pressure. This requires institutional slack—permission to delay, adjust, or question a process when it's not working. Systems without this capacity force workarounds underground, where they become fragile and dependent on individual heroics.

Working With Existing Systems

Most design happens inside systems we didn't build. We inherit procurement rules, staffing constraints, legacy technology, and political realities that shape what's possible. The question isn't how to design the ideal system from scratch, but how to work effectively within inherited constraints.

This requires staying with systems after launch. Understanding how they degrade, where they break under load, what workarounds emerge in practice. The temporal gap between design and consequence means that systems reveal their actual requirements slowly, through use.

Forward-deployed practitioners—people embedded in operational reality rather than strategy sessions—can observe these patterns and feed them back into organizational learning. This isn't heroic individual practice. It's building institutional capacity to learn from delivery.

Making Coordination Visible

Coordination costs often fall on whoever is closest to delivery—usually the people with least authority to change the system. Making these costs visible means documenting the actual work required to deliver a service:

  • How many approvals does a simple change need?
  • Who has to be in the loop for an exception?
  • What happens when someone is out sick?
  • Where do handoffs create delays or errors?

This documentation isn't bureaucracy for its own sake. It's understanding the system well enough to redesign it.

The Human Work Behind Automation

Every "streamlined" process depends on someone managing exceptions. Every "automated" system requires human judgment when rules don't fit reality. Content moderation, data cleaning, algorithmic bias correction—tasks that get positioned as edge cases but are actually core to operations.

The question isn't whether humans will be in the loop, but whether we design systems that support their judgment rather than burning them out.

Sustainable systems distribute care work fairly. They create structural space for people to exercise judgment, access expertise, and coordinate effectively. This is infrastructure—not in the sense of pipes and cables, but institutional capacity to maintain services over time.

Using This Framework

Public Mechanics offers diagnostic questions for any delivery system:

  • Where does repair work happen, and who does it?
  • What local knowledge keeps this system functional?
  • What coordination costs are invisible or falling on frontline workers?
  • Where does the system prevent people from doing what they know is right?
  • How does authority flow, and who can intervene when something breaks?

These questions apply whether you're working in government benefits, healthcare delivery, transit operations, or any system where policy meets implementation.

Get Started

  1. Document a workaround - Pick one system you work with. Write down how it's supposed to work, then how it actually works. What's the gap?
  2. Map coordination costs - For a recent change or exception, trace every step: who noticed, who escalated, who approved, who implemented, who documented, who trained others.
  3. Interview repair workers - Talk to the people who keep your system running. What do they know that isn't written down? What would break if they left?
  4. Make one thing visible - Take one invisible repair pattern and document it for decision-makers. Don't ask for resources yet—just make the work visible.
