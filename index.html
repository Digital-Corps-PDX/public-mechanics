Public Mechanics

A practice framework for understanding how public systems actually work

The Work That Keeps Things Running

Case workers bypass broken eligibility systems. Bus drivers wait for passengers they know need extra time. Teachers feed kids from personal supplies when meal programs fail. IT contractors patch the same permissions matrix weekly because procurement won't fund an audit.

This is repair work. It's also design work—the kind that happens after launch, when systems meet reality.

How to Use This Framework

Public Mechanics offers diagnostic questions for any delivery system:

  • Where does repair work happen, and who does it?
  • What local knowledge keeps this system functional?
  • What coordination costs are invisible or falling on frontline workers?
  • Where does the system prevent people from doing what they know is right?
  • How does authority flow, and who can intervene when something breaks?

Get Started

  1. Document a workaround — Pick one system. Write how it's supposed to work, then how it actually works. What's the gap?
  2. Map coordination costs — For a recent change, trace every step: who noticed, escalated, approved, implemented, documented, trained.
  3. Interview repair workers — Talk to people who keep your system running. What do they know that isn't written down?
  4. Make one thing visible — Document one invisible repair pattern for decision-makers. Don't ask for resources yet—just make the work visible.

Core Concepts

Systems Degrade, They Don't Just Fail

Most systems don't fail cleanly. They degrade through accumulated neglect, policy contradictions, and the gap between design and reality. The people keeping these systems functional develop sophisticated practices for working around institutional constraints.

Repair Requires Coordination

Every intervention creates coordination costs. Someone notices, escalates, gets permission, finds resources, communicates changes, updates documentation, trains others. These costs reflect who has power—who can stop a process, override a rule, or intervene when something breaks.

Local Knowledge Is Essential

Understanding how case workers actually process applications, how drivers manage routes under real conditions, how teachers navigate supply chain failures—this knowledge is often invisible to people who design, fund, or govern these systems. Yet it's essential for delivery.

Stay With Systems After Launch

Systems reveal their actual requirements slowly, through use. Forward-deployed practitioners—people embedded in operational reality—can observe patterns and feed them back into organizational learning.

Automation Depends on Human Judgment

Every "streamlined" process depends on someone managing exceptions. Every "automated" system requires human judgment when rules don't fit reality. The question isn't whether humans will be in the loop, but whether we design systems that support their judgment rather than burning them out.
